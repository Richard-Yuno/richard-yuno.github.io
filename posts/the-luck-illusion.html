The Luck Illusion: Why You Can't Tell Skill From Chance — And What That Costs You

A Nobel laureate watched flight instructors draw exactly the wrong lesson from their best students. A Wall Street strategist built a spectrum from roulette to chess. A trader-philosopher made a fortune betting that everyone else was fooled. They were all circling the same blind spot.

Curated & concluded by Richard Ngcamu · YUNO · 8 min read

Daniel Kahneman tells the story of teaching Israeli flight instructors about the psychology of training. The instructors objected: praise didn't work, they insisted — whenever they praised a cadet for a brilliant manoeuvre, the next attempt was worse. Punishment worked: scream at a cadet after a terrible landing, and the next one improved. The instructors had years of consistent evidence.

They were completely wrong, and the reason is one of the most important ideas in statistics: regression to the mean. An exceptionally good landing is partly skill and partly luck — and luck doesn't repeat. The next attempt was always likely to be closer to average, regardless of what the instructor said. The praise didn't cause the decline and the shouting didn't cause the improvement; chance did both, and the instructors built an entire theory of teaching — reward harshness, distrust kindness — on top of noise.

The skill–luck spectrum

Michael Mauboussin, the Columbia Business School adjunct and investment strategist, spent a career formalising this problem in The Success Equation. His test for how much luck an activity contains is elegant: ask whether you can lose on purpose. A chess grandmaster can lose deliberately — chess is nearly pure skill. A roulette player cannot — roulette is pure luck. Most of what matters in life — careers, investing, business, hiring, even publishing a blog post — sits awkwardly in the middle, and Mauboussin's data shows we systematically misjudge where. His most practical finding: the more luck an activity contains, the more you should judge decisions by the quality of the process, not the outcome — because in high-luck domains, good decisions routinely produce bad short-term results and vice versa.

In any game containing luck, the scoreboard lies in the short run. Only the process tells the truth.

Fooled, systematically

Nassim Taleb's Fooled by Randomness supplies the darker half of the picture. Financial markets, he argues, are machines for manufacturing false heroes: give ten thousand fund managers coin-flip odds for ten years, and dozens will boast perfect streaks through chance alone — and they will be the ones writing books about their method. The survivors are visible; the equally skilled failures are not. This survivorship bias means the success stories we study are a curated sample of the lucky, which is why imitating the habits of billionaires is such unreliable advice.

The Cornell economist Robert Frank closed the loop in Success and Luck with a finding about self-perception: the more successful people become, the less luck they remember. Hindsight rewrites the lottery as a plan. Frank's simulations show that in large competitive fields, the winner is almost never the most talented candidate — talent gets you into the final round; chance picks the champion. And experiments he cites show that merely reminding successful people of their luck makes them measurably more generous and better calibrated about risk.

What the blind spot costs

Put the four bodies of work together and the price of the illusion becomes concrete. We promote and invest on hot streaks that are noise (Kahneman). We copy the visible winners and inherit their risk, not their skill (Taleb). We judge our own good decisions harshly when luck goes against them, and abandon sound strategies one bad quarter too early (Mauboussin). And we grow overconfident precisely as our sample of good luck grows (Frank). The illusion flatters us in good times and gaslights us in bad ones.

The repair is the same in every study: separate the decision from the outcome, in writing, before the result arrives. Mauboussin prescribes decision journals; poker champion turned decision scientist Annie Duke calls outcome-worship "resulting" and considers it the amateur's defining error. A recorded reason cannot be rewritten by hindsight — which makes a cheap notebook the closest thing luck research offers to a superpower.

Sources consulted

  • Michael Mauboussin, The Success Equation: Untangling Skill and Luck (2012)

  • Robert H. Frank, Success and Luck: Good Fortune and the Myth of Meritocracy (2016)
  • Annie Duke, Thinking in Bets (2018)

The One Truth

You cannot feel the difference between skill and luck — nobody can. So stop judging yourself and others by outcomes, and start judging decisions by the process that produced them, recorded before the result is known. In the long run, process is the only scoreboard that doesn't lie.
